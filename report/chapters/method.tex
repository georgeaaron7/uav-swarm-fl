\chapter{Theoretical Study}

This chapter describes the simulated system, the attacker and the aggregation rules
compared, and analyses the reputation update. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} describes

Figure~\ref{fig:system} shows the system. $K$ UAVs are served by $B$ ground base
stations (BSs). Each UAV $k$ holds a private dataset $\mathcal{D}_k$ of $n_k$ images.
In every round $t$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item each UAV attached to BS $b$ starts from that BS's edge model $\mathbf{w}^t_b$
  and trains it for one local epoch of SGD on $\mathcal{D}_k$;
\item it uploads the update $\boldsymbol{\Delta}^t_k$ (new local model minus the edge
  model), which is split into packets of $L$ parameters;
\item the BS aggregates the updates it receives and sets
  $\mathbf{w}^{t+1}_b=\mathbf{w}^t_b+\mathrm{Agg}(\cdot)$;
\item every $E$ rounds the edge models are averaged, weighted by the number of
  training samples held by the UAVs in each cell, and the result is sent back to
  every BS.
\end{enumerate}
UAVs attach to the BS with the strongest received power. A UAV hands over only when
another BS is stronger by a hysteresis margin of 3\,dB for two consecutive rounds,
which avoids ping-pong handovers (\texttt{fl/topology.py}).

\subsection{Dataset and Model}
Images come from CIFAR-10, transformed to imitate some properties of aerial imagery
(\texttt{fl/data.py}); we call the result aerial-transformed CIFAR-10, since the
transformations do not make it genuine UAV imagery. Each image is rotated by a random multiple of
$90^\circ$, since a downward-looking camera has no preferred orientation, and with
probability 0.3 or 0.2 is down-sampled to $24\times24$ or $16\times16$ pixels and
up-sampled again to imitate higher flight altitudes. Training images are split among
the UAVs either uniformly (IID) or with Dirichlet($\alpha$) class proportions. In the
controlled study of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, 500 images (50 per class) are held out as
the root set $\mathcal{D}_0$, which is assumed clean and never given to any UAV, and
the remaining 49{,}500 are split among the UAVs. The earlier baseline experiments
split all 50{,}000 images among the UAVs and used no root set.

The classifier, TacticalEdgeCNN, is a small convolutional network with three
convolutional layers (16, 32 and 64 channels) and two fully connected layers,
156{,}074 trainable parameters in total and no normalization layers, chosen so that it
could plausibly be trained on board. Without normalization layers the whole model
state consists of plain weights, which keeps averaging across UAVs straightforward.

\section{Wireless Uplink Models}
\label{sec:channel}
The channel decides, for every packet of every update, whether it arrives
(\texttt{fl/channel.py}). Two models are used.

\subsection{Synthetic Jammed Channel}
UAVs move by a random walk at 100\,m altitude over an $800\times800$\,m area. The
received power from UAV $k$ at its serving BS is
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{rx},k} = P_{\mathrm{tx}} - \mathrm{PL}(d_k) + 10\log_{10}|h_k|^2,
  \qquad \mathrm{PL}(d)=40+10\eta\log_{10}d \ \text{dB},
\end{equation}
with $P_{\mathrm{tx}}=20$\,dBm, path-loss exponent $\eta=2.2$, and Rayleigh fading
$|h_k|^2\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)$. A ground jammer of power 10\,dBm targets a sector of
the swarm, and the SINR is
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_k = P_{\mathrm{rx},k} - 10\log_{10}\bigl(N_0 + J_k\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $N_0$ ($-90$\,dBm) and the received jamming power $J_k$ (zero for UAVs outside
the jammed sector) are added as linear powers in mW before conversion to dBm. This
jammed channel is used only in the preliminary study; the controlled study of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} has no jammer.

\subsection{Measured-Trace Channel}
For realism we replay a measured UAV link from DeepSense~6G
Scenario~23 \citep{alkhateeb2023deepsense,charan2025drone}, in which a drone flies
around a ground receiver that records 60\,GHz beam powers together with position,
altitude and speed. The dataset's power values are uncalibrated, so only their
variation relative to the median is used:
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_k = \bar\gamma + \bigl(P^{\mathrm{meas}}_k - \mathrm{med}\,P^{\mathrm{meas}}\bigr)
  - 10\eta\log_{10}\frac{d_{k,b}}{d_{k,1}},
\end{equation}
where $P^{\mathrm{meas}}_k$ is the recorded beam power in dB on the dataset's
arbitrary scale, $\bar\gamma$ is an assumed reference SNR, and the last term adjusts
the measured link to the distance of a virtual BS $b$, with distances in metres so
that their ratio is dimensionless. Each UAV replays a different flight
segment. Two settings are used: $\bar\gamma=15$\,dB, a good link, and
$\bar\gamma=5$\,dB, a weak link. They replay identical trajectories, so only the
link quality differs.

\subsection{Packet Erasures}
In both models, conditional on the current link state, packets are lost
independently; all $L$ parameters of a packet share one erasure. Each packet is lost
with probability
\begin{equation}
  p_k = \frac{1}{1+\exp\bigl(a(\gamma_k-\gamma_{\mathrm{th}})\bigr)},
\end{equation}
with $a=1$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}=3$\,dB. An update whose transmission time at
the Shannon rate exceeds the 2\,s round deadline is dropped entirely. The BS
therefore receives $\tilde{\boldsymbol\Delta}_k=\mathbf{m}_k\odot\boldsymbol\Delta_k$,
where the mask $\mathbf{m}_k$ is 1 for delivered coordinates and 0 elsewhere. The BS
also records link telemetry for each UAV: SNR, packet delivery ratio (PDR), long-run
reliability, distance, speed and handover state.

\subsubsection{Choice of the packet-error curve}
The base paper \citep{shirvanimoghaddam2022erroneous} models packet errors with the
normal approximation for finite-length codes: a $k$-bit packet sent in $n$ channel
uses at SNR $\gamma$ is lost with probability
\begin{equation}
  p = Q\!\left(\frac{n\log_2(1+\gamma)-k+\log_2 n}{\sqrt{n\,V(\gamma)}}\right),\qquad
  V(\gamma)=\bigl(1-(1+\gamma)^{-2}\bigr)\log_2^2 e .
  \label{eq:normal-approx}
\end{equation}
Our packets carry $L=1024$ parameters of 32 bits, so $k=\PerBits$. Choosing the code
rate whose capacity threshold is $\gamma_{\mathrm{th}}=3$\,dB, $R=\log_2(1+10^{0.3})
=\PerRate$ bits per channel use ($n\approx\PerUses$), places the 50\% point of
\eqref{eq:normal-approx} at 3\,dB, the midpoint of our logistic curve. For a single
codeword of this length, however, \eqref{eq:normal-approx} is a waterfall: the packet
error rate falls from 90\% to 10\% within \PerWaterfall\,dB. The SNR replayed from
the trace is one sample per round, while the real SNR varies during a round. If that
variation is modelled as a Gaussian spread in dB and \eqref{eq:normal-approx} is
averaged over it, a spread of \PerSpread\,dB reproduces the logistic curve with
$a=1$ to within \PerMaxGap{} percentage point of packet error rate at every SNR
(Fig.~\ref{fig:per-curve}). The logistic curve is therefore the base paper's model
with unresolved within-round SNR variation averaged in. The spread is a fitted
modelling assumption, not a measured value, and because the 5 and 15\,dB anchors
are also assumed, the results are best read against the delivery ratios they produce
(Section~\ref{sec:controlled}). Phase~II will use \eqref{eq:normal-approx} directly,
with the SNR variation taken from the trace.

\section{Threat Model}
A fixed set of $f=3$ of the $K=10$ UAVs is Byzantine. They follow the protocol (they
train, transmit on time and use the same channel) but replace their update with one
of the following (\texttt{fl/attacks.py}, \texttt{fl/client.py}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Label flip} (data poisoning): train on labels mapped $c\mapsto 9-c$.
\item \textbf{Sign flip}: send $-\lambda\boldsymbol\Delta_k$ with $\lambda=5$.
\item \textbf{ALIE-style} \citep{baruch2019alie}: send
  $\boldsymbol\mu-z\,\boldsymbol\sigma\odot\mathrm{sign}(\boldsymbol\mu)$ with $z=1.5$,
  where $\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\boldsymbol\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of
  the honest updates in the attacker's cell.
\item \textbf{IPM} \citep{xie2019ipm}: send $-\epsilon\boldsymbol\mu$ with $\epsilon=0.5$.
\end{itemize}
The ALIE and IPM attackers know the honest updates of their cell, which is a strong
assumption chosen to stress the defences. The BS's telemetry is assumed authentic;
attacks that forge it are left for Phase~II.

\section{Aggregation Rules}
All rules receive exactly the same inputs: the received updates, their masks, sample
counts and telemetry (\texttt{fl/aggregators.py}).

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:method-baselines}
\textbf{FedAvg} \citep{mcmahan2017fedavg} averages the received updates weighted by
$n_k$, with lost coordinates counted as zeros. \textbf{CosAvg}, used only in the
preliminary study, is a CosAvg-style baseline inspired by the earlier UAV-FL work of
\citet{liao2023tee}: it weights each update by
$\max(0,\cos(\boldsymbol\Delta_k,\bar{\boldsymbol\Delta}))\,n_k$, where
$\bar{\boldsymbol\Delta}$ is the FedAvg mean. It is not an exact reproduction of that
paper, whose Algorithm~2 sums pairwise cosine similarities and aggregates the
top-ranked updates.

\textbf{Coordinate-wise median} \citep{yin2018median} and \textbf{FLTrust}
\citep{cao2021fltrust} were defined for complete update vectors; we adapt both to the
packet masks. The median takes, for each parameter, the median of the delivered
values only (the lower middle value when their number is even). FLTrust computes a
root update $\mathbf{r}$ on $\mathcal{D}_0$ with as many local steps as the median
UAV in the cell, weights each UAV by $\max(0,\cos)$ to $\mathbf{r}$ on its delivered
coordinates, rescales the delivered part to the norm of $\mathbf{r}$ on the same
coordinates, and averages each coordinate over the UAVs that delivered it. The
root-set design is FLTrust's; the mask handling is our adaptation.

\textbf{Base-paper erasure rules.} The base paper \citep{shirvanimoghaddam2022erroneous}
models each upload as erased or received and compares two server rules: average only
the fresh updates received in the round (its Eq.~(4)), or reuse the last local
parameters received from a client whose fresh update is missing (its Eq.~(5)). We
implement both at packet granularity, which lets them use every delivered packet:
\emph{fresh-only} averages each coordinate over the UAVs that delivered it, weighted by
$n_k$; \emph{reuse-old} fills each lost coordinate, and every coordinate of a fully
erased upload, with the last parameter value received from that UAV, re-expressed
against the current edge model. Neither rule defends against attackers, as the base
paper assumes none.

\subsection{Context-Calibrated Robust Aggregation (CCRA)}
CCRA is the aggregator designed in this project. Each BS applies the following
steps every round.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Reference update.} Compute $\mathbf{r}$ on $\mathcal{D}_0$, as in FLTrust.
  Our diagnostics showed that, under non-IID data, the mean or median of the UAV
  updates is not a reliable reference: attackers and honest UAVs with skewed data
  had similar cosine similarity to it.
\item \textbf{Norm clipping.} Let $\rho_k$ be the RMS of the delivered entries of
  $\tilde{\boldsymbol\Delta}_k$ and scale the update by
  $s_k=\min(1,\ \mathrm{med}_j\rho_j/\rho_k)$, with a small constant added to
  $\rho_k$ so that a zero update is handled. This limits the effect of large-norm
  updates.
\item \textbf{Masked similarity.}
  $c_k=\cos\bigl(\mathcal{S}(s_k\tilde{\boldsymbol\Delta}_k;\mathbf{m}_k),\
  \mathcal{S}(\mathbf{r};\mathbf{m}_k)\bigr)$, where $\mathcal{S}(\cdot;\mathbf{m})$
  keeps only the coordinates with $\mathbf{m}=1$, and $c_k=0$ for an empty mask or a
  zero vector. This removes the artifact of lost packets counted as zeros; it does not
  guarantee that the missing coordinates never bias the comparison.
\item \textbf{Behaviour score.} $b_k=\mathrm{clip}\bigl(c_k/\mathrm{med}_{j:c_j>0}c_j,\
  0,\ 1\bigr)$. Updates pointing away from the reference get $b_k=0$, and all scores
  are 0 if no $c_j$ is positive.
\item \textbf{Reputation.} With memory $\beta\in[0,1)$, initial trust 1 and evidence
  $e_k\in[0,1]$,
  \begin{equation}
    T_k \leftarrow T_k+\eta_k(b_k-T_k),\qquad
    \eta_k=\begin{cases}(1-\beta)\,e_k, & b_k>T_k,\\ 1-\beta, & b_k\le T_k.\end{cases}
    \label{eq:reputation}
  \end{equation}
  Penalties apply at full rate, so an attacker cannot hide by dropping its own
  packets; only rewards are slowed on poor links.
\item \textbf{Gating and averaging.} UAVs with $T_k<\tau$ are excluded. The others get
  weight $\omega_k=n_kT_k$, discounted for staleness, and each coordinate is averaged
  only over the UAVs that delivered it (a coordinate with no accepted weight gets a
  zero update):
  \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Agg}_p=\frac{\sum_k\omega_k\,m_{k,p}\,s_k\tilde\Delta_{k,p}}
                      {\sum_k\omega_k\,m_{k,p}}.
    \label{eq:masked-average}
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The evidence term multiplies five link factors:
\begin{equation}
  e_k=\mathrm{PDR}_k\cdot R_k\cdot h_k\cdot\frac{1}{1+d_k/A}\cdot\frac{1}{1+|v_k|},
\end{equation}
namely the packet delivery ratio, long-run reliability, a handover factor
($h_k=0.5$ during a handover, 1 otherwise), a distance factor with $d_k$ the
UAV--BS distance and $A=800$\,m the configured side length of the area, and a factor
in the dimensionless normalized speed $|v_k|$.

\subsection{Ablation Variants}
To find out which part of CCRA matters, three variants are compared.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CCRA (full)}: all steps, with evidence $e_k$ from telemetry.
\item \textbf{Mask-only}: $e_k=1$, but the masks are used in steps 2--6.
\item \textbf{No-context}: $e_k=1$ and $\mathbf{m}_k=\mathbf{1}$, so lost
  coordinates enter the computation as zeros, as a channel-unaware server would see
  them.
\end{itemize}
Server-side deadline handling and staleness treatment are shared by all variants; only
the mechanisms listed above are ablated, so no-context is not a channel-free system.
The code also has an option to correct $c_k$ for the noise expected at the
reported SINR. It is inactive in the controlled study, which adds no residual noise
to delivered parameters, so the full-versus-mask-only contrast isolates $e_k$.

\subsection{Analysis of the Reputation Update}
\label{sec:fixed-point}
The asymmetric update~\eqref{eq:reputation} has a consequence that was not intended.
Suppose a UAV's evidence is constant, $e_k=e\in(0,1]$, its scores $b\in[0,1]$ are
i.i.d.\ and independent of its current reputation, with $0<\mathbb{E}[b]<1$, and
$0\le\beta<1$. Conditional on the current level $T$, the expected change in reputation
is
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[\Delta T\mid T] = (1-\beta)\,g_e(T),\qquad
  g_e(T)=e\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(b-T)^+\bigr]-\mathbb{E}\bigl[(T-b)^+\bigr],
\end{equation}
so the drift vanishes where
\begin{equation}
  e\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[(b-T^\star)^+\bigr] = \mathbb{E}\bigl[(T^\star-b)^+\bigr].
  \label{eq:fixed-point}
\end{equation}
This balance is an expectile condition \citep{newey1987expectile}: dividing by $1+e$
gives $q\,\mathbb{E}[(b-T^\star)^+]=(1-q)\,\mathbb{E}[(T^\star-b)^+]$ with
$q=e/(1+e)$, the first-order condition that defines the $q$-expectile of $b$. Our
contribution is to apply this relationship to the reputation rule.

\noindent\textbf{Proposition 3.1.} Under the assumptions above, $g_e$ has a unique
zero $T^\star(e)\in(0,1)$, the $q$-expectile of $b$ with $q=e/(1+e)$. It equals
$\mathbb{E}[b]$ when $e=1$, is nondecreasing in $e$, and tends to
$\operatorname{ess\,inf} b$ as $e$ decreases to zero.

\noindent\textit{Proof.} $g_e$ is continuous with $g_e(0)=e\,\mathbb{E}[b]>0$ and
$g_e(1)=-\mathbb{E}[1-b]<0$, so a zero exists in $(0,1)$. For $T_2>T_1$, the change
$g_e(T_2)-g_e(T_1)$ lies between $-(T_2-T_1)$ and $-e(T_2-T_1)$, so $g_e$ is strictly
decreasing and the zero is unique. For $e=1$, $g_1(T)=\mathbb{E}[b]-T$. Increasing $e$
weakly increases $g_e(T)$ for every $T$, so it cannot lower the zero. For
$T<\operatorname{ess\,inf}b$, the downward term vanishes and $g_e(T)>0$, so the zero
is at least $\operatorname{ess\,inf}b$. For any larger $T$, the downward term
$\mathbb{E}[(T-b)^+]$ is strictly positive while the upward term
$e\,\mathbb{E}[(b-T)^+]$ vanishes as $e\to0$, so the limiting zero cannot exceed
$\operatorname{ess\,inf}b$. \hfill$\blacksquare$

$T^\star$ is the equilibrium of the conditional drift, not a statement that the
reputation converges almost surely: with a constant step ($\beta=0.5$) and random
scores it keeps fluctuating, and in general $\mathbb{E}[g_e(T)]\neq g_e(\mathbb{E}[T])$.
In the simulator the evidence also changes over time, and scores depend on training,
on the other UAVs and on the median normalization, so the constant-evidence, i.i.d.\
assumptions are an explanatory approximation. Under that approximation, weakening the
evidence lowers the equilibrium from the mean score toward the lower end of the score
distribution. A finite weak evidence does not put it at the lowest score; that is
only the limit as $e\to0$. An honest UAV with skewed data has noisy scores, so on a
weak link this suggests a mechanism by which its trust recovers more slowly and falls
below the gate $\tau$ more often. An attacker's scores are already near zero, so
slowing its rewards adds little. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} checks whether the
measured gate decisions are consistent with this mechanism.
